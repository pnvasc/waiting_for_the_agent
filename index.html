Two axes: how long the wait is, and how legible the process is.

Hsieh punched a clock every hour for a year — each wait short, and perfectly legible; you know exactly what is coming.

Bread: long, and legible. You have a model, so you can set an alarm and really leave.

Blood test results: long, and illegible. You have no model of the process at all — but it is long enough to release you.

The agent: short, and illegible. The only corner with neither.

Shortness produces captivity. Illegibility produces scatter & anxiety.

The agent gets both.

From the essay: "The wait is too short to leave. And being of unknown length makes it too expensive."

Even an indeterminate wait that is known to be long releases me. I can log off. Here I have to stay reachable — so whatever I fill the time with has to be easy to abandon, which means it can't be worth entering fully.

Heidegger: the hammer withdraws while it hammers. Here the waiting is constitutive of the tool's operation, and it is the waiting that makes the work break down into view.

Waiting

queuing is the allocation of scarce access

Barry Schwartz, "Queues, Priorities, and Social Process". Queues can be comprised of anything — from people to tasks.

He invites us to view social systems as queuing networks, and to look at the demands queues make on those who serve them, not only on those who wait.

By working with agents I start new tasks, in part because it is so frictionless.

I start new activities that create a queue where I am the bottleneck.

Schwartz's unintuitive point: organisations create efficiency by introducing bottlenecks — the secretary triaging access to the bureaucrat who makes the consequential decisions.

I "increase my productivity" by generating new branches of tasks. So I can be thought of as a queuing network, and the queuing of tasks is the task of allocating scarce access to me.

And the punchline, which may want its own slide: By waiting, I create new opportunities for waiting.

[PAULA: 1968 vs 2026 — to expand]

Robert B. Miller, 1968, "Response time in man-computer conversational transactions". Surprising that this was already being thought about then.

Roughly 2.3 to 3.5 seconds registers as "present"; after that, everything is "later". Past 15 seconds, attention to focused problem-solving degrades and diverts to other tasks. It also curbs exploration — long waits make us satisfied with the first result.

Miller's engineer wrote the program and is invested in its working. That is me running PhD simulations — the horror of a long run that ended in failure. With the agent the stakes are dislocated; there is less of "me" in there, so I barely wait 15 seconds before moving on.

Achievability and goal-proximity bias the choice of the next course of action

Neumann and Allport, "Beyond capacity: a functional view of attention". Attention is not a scarce resource to be rationed; it is selection for activities that maintain behavioural coherence, because there is always more to do than can be done.

Action selection is primed for what looks achievable now. So the next thing is also in front of the PC. It is another agent.

switching between different activities often requires just as much effort as continuing to do one and the same activity

Jelle Bruineberg, "Adversarial inference: predictive minds in the attention economy". "Adversarial" refers to platforms that model users to keep them there — but here nobody needs to model us. Frictionless switching plus undefined waits produces the same dynamics without anybody modelling us.

Rest? No.

a more tyrannical version of Taylor’s manager has been internalized by me to such an extent that no one needs to oversee my use of time

Taylorism argued against worker-controlled idle time in favour of managerially controlled idle time — it prescribed rest as much as it prescribed efficient work, so long as management decided when.

Looking at my malaise over a few idle seconds: this might have been one of the great achievements of productivity culture in the 20th century.

[PAULA: ending]

The promise: AI will make us productive and give us leisure, in Russell's sense. It won't replace us, it will free us.

Godot never comes, and he can't ever come — the play would end. Didi and Gogo are trapped in time, replaying the same sequences like liturgy, the way the Church built daily ritual around the indefinite wait for the Messiah.

The small acts, the little recursions, are what make the large arrival impossible by perpetually postponing it. Relief never comes because recursion has no in-built stop mechanism.
